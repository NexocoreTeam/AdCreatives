Part 1 · The Framework
Vol. 01
— Why TOFU / MOFU / BOFU stopped working

The traditional funnel tells you where someone is in your media system. It tells you almost nothing about where they are inside their own head.

The TOFU / MOFU / BOFU framework was built around media-buying logic, not customer psychology. It describes where someone sits in your ad system — not what's actually happening inside their head while they're making the buying decision. That framework worked for a while. It doesn't anymore.

Three things have changed:

  • Meta's algorithm runs on broad targeting now. You don't control who sees what. The platform optimizes against signals you can't see and audiences you can't define. The traditional funnel assumes you can — and that assumption costs brands money.
  • Creative diversity is the new lever. With targeting flattened, the creative itself does the work the audience selection used to do.
  • Performance language has decoupled from creative logic. "Top of funnel" tells you nothing about what to put inside the frame. A Trigger-stage buyer and an Evaluation-stage buyer might both be "cold" — they need completely different messages.
"
If you're constantly discounting to close the sale, the problem is that your Trigger, Exploration, and Evaluation creative didn't do its job. The discount becomes a crutch — and an expensive one.
Discounts wallpaper over the gap. They don't close it. The 60-day money-back guarantee removes financial risk for someone who's already made the psychological decision — it's a Purchase-stage tool, not a substitute for the work the earlier stages need to do.

This framework is baked into how we generate every brief — each one tagged to one of the four mental stages.

What replaces it — a framework built on how the buyer's brain actually decides, not where they sit in your ad system — is what the rest of this document is about.

— The four paired-mechanism patterns

For this cohort, four pairings repeat. Use these. Avoid the rest.

Across the six personas, four mechanism pairings keep surfacing as the recommended creative architecture. Each pairs an analytical lever (the proof, the data, the mechanism) with an emotional or framing lever (the reframe, the trigger, the felt experience). The cohort's authority + framing dominance shows up in every one.

01

First-principles + Loss aversion

Mechanism reveal plus framing as cost of inaction.

The category-indictment pairing. Authority + framing — the cohort's exact heuristic stack. The buyer sees the structural failure mechanism and the cost of staying with what's failing in the same beat.

Recommended for: Danielle, Paul, Paula, Brandon.

02

Reframing + Emotional trigger

Frame flip plus the felt experience.

The "you can stop fighting this" pairing. The buyer sees her own experience mirrored back, then receives the reframe as relief — not as a sales pitch, as recognition.

Recommended for: Isaac, Natalie.

03

Authority borrowing + Data insight

Credentialed third-party voice plus a specific stat.

The analytical-persona pairing. A practitioner, researcher, or peer of credibility delivers the receipt — the trial number, the duration, the named outcome. The brand recedes; the evidence leads.

Recommended for: Isaac, Paul, Brandon.

04

Counterintuitive insight + Specificity

Pattern break plus the receipt.

The "literature ahead of practice" pairing. The opening line subverts a category assumption ("your doctor hasn't mentioned this yet, and there's a reason"), then the next line delivers the specific evidence that makes the subversion defensible.

Recommended for: Paula, Brandon.

Patterns we never use
These violate the cohort's heuristic profile

Gamification + time-sensitive offer

Urgency theater violates the cohort. Countdown timers, "today only," scarcity gates — every persona reads them as manipulation, not motivation. These buyers have already been burned by exactly this move in the probiotic category.

Shock factor + transformation shortcut

"Miracle," "revolutionary," "life-changing" — all on the brand's prohibited-terms list for a reason. The cohort is allergic to hype. Every claim that promises a transformation without naming the mechanism gets discounted as marketing theater.

Curiosity + reverse psychology

Cleverness violates the brand's directness. The bold voice is wry, not coy. "You'll never guess what's actually in your probiotic" loses to "~70% died before they reached your gut" every time.

— Six personas, one core argument, six registers

Same bottle. Same brand. Six entirely different hooks.

Writing one ad for "the gut-health audience" is the most common failure mode in this space. The buyer who responds to "you optimized everything" is a different person from the buyer who responds to "six months postpartum and your gut still doesn't feel like yours." Below: snapshot, what they want and fear, psychology triggers, hooks that land, and the do's and don'ts that govern each one.

01

Burnout Biohacker Brandon Primary

Snapshot. 30–45, performance-coded, tracks HRV, runs a disciplined supplement stack. Already takes a refrigerated multi-strain probiotic. Accepts inconsistent digestion and afternoon cognitive dips as "unsolved variables" — probably stress, probably sleep debt, probably aging. Wants: measurable improvement, stack simplification, evidence he can google later. Fears: adding another supplement that doesn't earn its place; being seen as naive.

Quadrant. LV/LI — quiet dissatisfaction with a stack that hasn't moved his metrics. Key move: completion, not replacement. "You bought wrong" kills the sale. "You were missing the active compound the bacteria were supposed to produce" earns it.

Triggers: authority bias · framing effect · first-principles thinking

"You optimized everything. Except the part where ~70% of your probiotic never reached your gut."
"Twelve months of a premium probiotic. Zero change in HRV. Zero change in digestion. You weren't doing it wrong. The product was."
"Your probiotic didn't earn its place in your stack. Give Gut Balance 60 days. It will."
Do
  • Lead with mechanism, not outcome promise
  • First-principles language ("delivery model failure," "stacked failure points")
  • Frame as stack rationalization, not stack addition
  • Cite study design (12-week RCT, 84-day clinical) when proof is the job
Don't
  • Suggest he was naive — frame his probiotic use as "incomplete data," not "you fell for it"
  • Use scarcity or urgency. Reads as manipulation.
  • Use transformation language ("the new you")
  • Rely on aspirational testimonials. Peer-coded only.
02

Perimenopause Paula Secondary

Snapshot. 42–55. Bloating, brain fog, digestive irregularity that worsened around perimenopause. Has been told her labs are fine. Has tried premium probiotics; nothing changed. Quietly skeptical of the medical system that hasn't given her an explanation. Wants: a real explanation; gut-hormone mechanism; quality of life. Fears: another supplement that disappoints; being told her symptoms aren't real.

Quadrant. LV/LI baseline, spiking to LV/HI before vacations, professional photos, or social events. Key move: her structural objection is "if it's hormonal, gut products can't fix it." The mechanism reframe — perimenopause depletes postbiotic output independent of probiotic input — is mandatory before any product mention.

Triggers: authority bias · framing effect

"Nobody told you that estrogen decline reshapes your gut microbiome. Your bloating isn't just hormonal. It's structural."
"You eat clean. You take the refrigerated probiotic. You still feel bloated by dinner and foggy by 10 AM. The problem isn't you."
"The last three supplements that promised to fix this cost you real money and nothing changed. Here's what's different."
Do
  • Acknowledge the institutional trust gap without bashing doctors
  • Use "literature ahead of practice" framing
  • Validate her years of disappointment as rational
  • Lead with the gut-hormone mechanism
Don't
  • Use aesthetic or body-appearance framing
  • Use scarcity or urgency
  • Manufacture excitement. Her position is cautious, not enthusiastic.
  • Lean on social proof from younger women. Peer-coded only.

Part 5 · The Mental Stages, Applied
Vol. 01
— Four stages. Four jobs.

Each stage has a job. The job determines the message, the hook, the proof points, and what to leave out.

01

Trigger

The customer wasn't looking for a supplement. Now they're looking for an explanation.

Job: surface the mechanism failure as a hidden variable behind a felt experience. Name the experience first ("clean eating all day, bloated by dinner"). Reveal the structural failure (the 70% transit-mortality stat). Do not mention the product. CTA: "see why."

"You eat clean. You still look three months pregnant by 7 PM. Stop blaming dinner."
Leave out:   product, ingredients, brand promise, trial offer.
02

Exploration

They know they have a problem. They're learning the category.

Job: establish postbiotics as a structurally distinct mechanism — not a rebrand, not a marketing pivot, an actual different thing. Lead with the mechanism distinction. First-principles framing. Brand visible, product secondary. CTA: "see how this works."

"Probiotics send live bacteria and hope they arrive. Postbiotics send what those bacteria were supposed to make."
Leave out:   discount, scarcity, testimonial-led proof, body claims.
03

Evaluation

They have a short list. They're looking for permission to choose.

Job: give the buyer decision confidence. Lead with proof points — study designs, durations, named outcomes. Peer-coded social proof. Address the persona's specific objection. CTA: "see the trials."

"Three separate human clinical trials. 17% fewer sick days. 84-day improvement in GI and quality of life. 30-day regularity outcomes."
Leave out:   heavy product hero shots without claims attached, generic aspirational testimonials.
04

Purchase

The decision is made. They're scanning for reasons not to act.

Job: remove the last friction. Lead with the 60-day money-back guarantee. Acknowledge the buyer's exhaustion. Short, calm, declarative — no urgency theater. CTA: "Start your 60-day risk-free trial."

"Try Gut Balance for 60 days. If your gut doesn't feel different, we'll give you your money back."
Leave out:   mechanism explanations (job is done), new claims, manufactured urgency.
"
Even within a single problem-aware persona, briefs should span all four mental stages. The buyer cycles. Done-Everything Danielle is in Trigger mode at 7 PM after dinner — and in Evaluation mode the next morning scrolling LinkedIn. Same persona, different head.

A note on ingredient names
EpiCor · Bereum · Totipro

Avoid leading hooks with patented compound names. For four of the six personas (Danielle, Natalie, Paula, and most of Isaac's surface area), they read as marketing jargon and trigger the exact skepticism we're working to disarm. Across hundreds of customer reviews and comments, real buyers reach for the meta-signal of evidence ("clinically studied," "publication," "data") — not specific compound names. The patented names work as trust receipts in body copy, on landing pages, and as on-package badges. The hook should be the outcome ("17% fewer sick days in a 12-week RCT"); the ingredient name belongs in the footnote.

The exceptions: Brandon and Paul both pattern-match positively on patented compound names as authority signals, even when they don't immediately recognize them. For those two personas specifically, naming Bereum or EpiCor in body copy strengthens the case.

Part 8 · The Four Biggest Levers
Vol. 01
— If we strip everything down

Four levers carry the most weight.

If we reduce the entire strategy to the moves most likely to produce conversions in Phase 1, four show up. Each is structural — competitors cannot easily retrofit any of them without abandoning their existing claims — and each converts more than one persona simultaneously.

01

Vindication before conversion

The single biggest lever.

Every persona except Natalie sits at the intersection of frustration plus identity-threat about "being the kind of person who falls for marketing." The bold variant exonerates them before selling — "you weren't wrong, the product was." That move satisfies the analytical personas (framing effect) AND the affective personas (effect heuristic via felt validation). One arc, two cohorts.

02

Mechanism reframe with a receipt in the next line

The category's failure mode is hype without receipts.

The bold voice rule is "every claim is backed by a receipt in the next line." This is the strongest structural differentiator from the competitive set because it cannot be retrofitted — competitors would have to abandon their existing claims to adopt it. Mechanism leads. Outcome follows. Named trial closes.

03

Body indictment that lands on the mechanism, not the body

Bloating is the cohort's number-one pain.

The bold variant has unique permission to attack it because the mechanism reframe redirects causality from her body to the product — "that's not you, that's the die-off." — First moves

Three paths give us the highest leverage out of the gate.

Each one tests a different load-bearing claim of the bold variant. Combined, they cover the four mental stages and the cohort's two dominant emotional quadrants.

Path A · Ground truth

The vindication static.

Stage: Exploration. Persona: Danielle. Format: typography-only static.

"You weren't wrong. The product was."

Why it leads. The bold thesis compressed into eight words. Runs the entire four-beat arc in a single line. Lowest possible production cost — three font-and-color variants, no shoot. The cleanest test of the brand voice's load-bearing claim: that buyers respond to category-indictment plus exoneration faster than they respond to product benefits.

Path B · Mechanism

The 70% reveal.

Stage: Exploration. Persona: Brandon or Danielle. Format: short-form video, mechanism walkthrough or visual literalization (capsule dissolving in acid).

"~70% of your probiotic never reached your gut. Here's the math."

Why it leads. The brand's most underweighted stage and its most defensible message. Performs the category-indictment job without naming the product. Works as cold creative without sounding like an ad.

Path C · Receipts

The evaluation card.

Stage: Evaluation. Persona: Danielle or Paula. Format: UGC-style testimonial-review hybrid with mechanism overlay, or clinical-summary card.

"Three probiotic bottles. Felt nothing. You weren't doing it wrong."

Why it leads. Cuts to the receipts in line two — 17% fewer sick days (12-week RCT), 84-day human clinical study, 30-day regularity outcomes. Where most personas currently sit, and where the brand has the strongest evidence stack in the category.
